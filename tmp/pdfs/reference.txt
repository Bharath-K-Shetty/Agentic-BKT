Abstract—Video-conferencing platforms have come a 
long way in their capability to encode-decode and render 
video/audio streams in real-time, but have not evolved 
nearly so far in their ability to support the participants of 
such a conference in capturing and retaining the decisions 
made during the conference in such a meeting. Meeting 
attendees are left to their own devices - taking notes or 
asking for confirmations and re-listening to what other 
participants said when a disagreement occurs. This paper 
describes the design of a proposed Smart Meeting 
Platform that aims to address this shortcoming through 
building an event-driven dashboard that uses low-latency 
automatic 
speech 
recognition 
(ASR), 
incremental 
abstractive summarization, and intent-based moderation 
of the conversation as its core features under well-defined 
API contracts. We identify four design goals that 
motivate and drive the proposal: < 2s glass-to-glass 
latency, transcription word error rate (WER) < 10%, 
ROUGE-L score of summaries > 0.45, and moderation 
F1-score > 0.95. We describe the microservice 
architecture of the proposed system, discuss the sliding-
window transformer approach used by the summarizer to 
allow incremental processing of the utterances without 
waiting for the end of the event, analyze the self-attention 
mechanism used as the mathematical foundation of this 
approach, describe the security and privacy assurances 
provided by the system, and walk through the evaluation 
methodology based on the AMI Meeting Corpus, 
LibriSpeech, and Jigsaw Toxic Comment datasets. In 
each case, we highlight the specific design choices that 
differentiate our solution in terms of lower latencies, 
protection of privacy, and overall contextual accuracy.

I. INTRODUCTION 
Hybrid and remote work has turned online meetings from an 
occasional 
convenience 
into 
the 
central 
mode 
of 
communication for organizations. The audio and video parts 
of that transition have been solved - except for the rare 
dropped connection, the latency of the media stream itself is 
rarely an issue. What has not kept up with the adoption of 
online meetings is everything that has to happen after the call 
- the tools do not really help much with organizing, 
remembering, and acting on the contents of a call. Anybody 
who has ever been on a ninety-minute planning call knows 
why a break is necessary, and why trying to do notes while 
listening is rarely effective - it does not let one do either. 
Another symptom of similar organizational shortcomings is 
observed at scale - when meetings grow beyond a certain size, 
the responsibility of a single moderator to enforce civility in 
real-time becomes too great to bear. 
The system described in this paper is meant to be a 
complement to conference platforms - it does not attempt to 
replace them, but rather offers to run alongside, using the 
audio tracks from the calls in real-time to enable speech-to-
text conversion and post-processing. It highlights uses of 
unprofessional language privately to the meeting host or 
offers a summary of the call’s contents in real-time. The 
system’s inference pipeline is based on the Sequence-to-
Sequence and Transformer architecture [2]; the paper details 
the necessary modifications to apply it to the streaming 
scenario. 
There are four main goals that the paper states for the 
described system, most of which are related to the system’s 
performance. First and foremost, it aims for less than two 
seconds of end-to-end latency for any given utterance for 
both transcription and summarization. Second, the system’s 
transcription accuracy should be higher than 90%, or, 
equivalently, have a lower than 10% Word Error Rate (WER) 
[1,7,10]. It aims for ROUGE-L higher than 0.45 for 
summaries compared to human-produced ones [6], and an 
F1-score higher than 0.95 for the moderation system, 
highlighting terms that should presumably be filtered in a 
professional conversation [11]. Each of these goals is 
discussed in detail in Sections II through IX, with each later 
section assessing the design choices’ ability to fulfill the 
stated promises. 
The motivation for this work is, unsurprisingly, derived from 
the problems that it attempts to solve. There are real 
applications for a system that can summarize conversations 
in video conferences because organizations now rely on them 
for communication, and remembering what was decided in 
which meeting is not always trivial. The most obvious 
solutions (assigning a person to take notes or asking to review 
the recording) are not particularly convenient - and one’s time 
spent on note-taking is, in the case of the recording review, 
wasted on something a machine could have done 
automatically. A human being would take longer than a 
machine to process the same material, despite being able to 
understand it better at the comprehension stage - the main 
point of taking notes by a person in the first place. The paper, 

therefore, represents the authors’ motivation for seeking a 
technology to reduce organizational overhead and bridge the 
gap between the tools that organizations have and the 
outcomes that they desire. 
The rest of the paper is structured around the aforementioned 
four goals. While the implementation takes a form of various 
models, pipelines, and microservices, each of the four 
performance targets described comprises a section dedicated 
to elaborating on the design choices. Specifically, Section II 
describes the work and research done for this paper and the 
four areas of this system that required special attention. It is 
followed by Section III, which narrows the scope to the 
problem at hand and states the assumptions that the 
implementation is based on. Then, Section IV describes the 
system’s microservice architecture in greater detail and 
proceeds to estimate the privacy and security implications of 
the system in Section V, addressing them as a design choice 
rather than an implementation detail. Section VI derives the 
mathematical expressions that the summarization module is 
based on, while Section VII describes the evaluation strategy. 
Finally, Section VIII discusses the design’s risks and 
opportunities, comparing this approach to a hypothetical 
alternative, and Section IX describes the implementation and 
deployment strategy. 
II. RELATED WORK 
Four of the approaches considered for the design of our 
solution were inspired by existing meeting analytics 
practices, all of which had some deficiencies in terms of real-
time processing, on-device implementation, and/or cost-
effectiveness. 
A. Post-hoc Transformer Summarization 
Typically, summarization algorithms based on large 
pretrained language models, such as BERT or GPT, require 
full transcripts to be delivered prior to generating a summary, 
which does not align with the immediacy of the dashboard 
under consideration [3,4,28]. One possible solution to this 
problem, sliding window summarization, is based on 
incremental processing of the input stream [29,30]. 
B. Cloud-based Speech and Sentiment APIs 
Using remote APIs for either automatic speech recognition 
(ASR) or sentiment analysis introduces extra network latency 
and potential privacy concerns, as all meeting content has to 
be sent to third-party servers for processing [23]. The 
dashboard under consideration seeks to avoid these 
drawbacks by relying on on-device processing, which, 
however, requires model quantization to effectively utilize 
available CPU/GPU resources [1,15]. 
C. Deep Neural Diarization 
Deep learning approaches to meeting diarization typically 
require significant computational resources and are used in a 
post-hoc fashion, which rules out their utilization in real-time 
applications, such as the dashboard under consideration 
[8,18]. As an alternative, the design under consideration 
employs a lightweight neural network to separate meeting 
participants by speaker using embeddings with minimal loss 
in performance [19]. 
D. Rule-based Moderation 
Although easy to implement, keyword filtering suffers from 
a significant number of false positives due to the inability to 
capture conversational context, thus requiring extensive 
customization [20,21]. The dashboard under consideration 
uses a pretrained DistilBERT classifier [5] to mitigate this 
issue, which was further fine-tuned on the Jigsaw Toxic 
Comment dataset to achieve an acceptable balance between 
precision and recall [11]. 
E. Commercial Meeting-Intelligence Products 
The meeting intelligence literature has witnessed the 
appearance of a number of commercial products addressing 
similar use cases [16,17]. Typically, they take the form of 
plugins to existing video conferencing software, offering 
speech-to-text transcription and post-hoc summarization at 
the level of individual utterances. Notably, they are generally 
based on the same principles as their academic counterparts 
described above [22]. As such, they are ill-suited for real-time 
analytics due to their reliance on cloud infrastructure. 
Moreover, their moderation capabilities are generally limited 
to profanity filtering at the lexical level without consideration 
of dialog context. By analyzing commercial products, one 
may actually get more insights into the set of functionalities 
that are typically considered appropriate by the target 
audience at a given point in time.

III. PROBLEM DEFINITION AND SCOPE 
Three problems, each with slightly different roots, motivate 
us initially. First is an analytic one, as meetings are not 
analyzed in the moment, requiring substantial winnowing of 
the long recording to identify what actually took place. 
Second, and perhaps more importantly, is the personal one, 
in that few are adept at simultaneously both receiving and 
recording what is being said in the meeting. Third, as 
meetings scale, a human moderator cannot focus sufficiently 
on all parts of the conversation, or on identifying toxic or 
unacceptable speech; platforms currently offer little help. 
With this said, we narrow our scope: our work is ongoing, 
and we save most of the broader meeting analytics tasks for 
later work. For now, our system analyzes live audio streams 
(WebRTC) [12], identifies who is speaking via standard 
diarization [8], and summarizes each speaker’s utterances 
using the sliding window transformer described in Section II 
[29,30]; moderation is implemented in the form of a 
dashboard for the meeting host, who is charged with 
enforcing standards. The system is optimized to minimize 
glass-to-glass latency, rather than focus on downstream 
analysis of hours-long recordings. 
We will note several simplifications that we make, which are 
justified both by practical limitations in the prototype and the 
desire to minimize risks, including focusing on one language, 
and only on meetings held in reasonably clean noise 
environments. We will describe our design choices, focusing 
on the three user roles outlined above. 
As mentioned, we consider three user roles, all of which are 
relevant at any given meeting. To begin with, a meeting host 
always has the dual purpose of focusing on the conversation 
itself, but also on ensuring that the conversation follows the 
desired trajectory. Other members can benefit from the ability 
to receive and digest summaries, but should not be distracted 
from the conversation. Moderation and summarization are 
available to everyone, but it is for the host to decide whether 
to use them and how to configure them to achieve the desired 
outcome. A third role is that of an IT or a compliance officer: 
they should be able to receive reports on all meetings, with 
audio attached if needed, without needing to be present for 
any of them. Separating the roles is critical to enabling each 
role’s capabilities while limiting the risks: for instance, it is 
essential that no one role has too much power. 
Summarization is not a weapon, but it becomes one if the 
wrong person has administrative access. Similarly, 
moderation needs to be available to everyone, but it is critical 
that it cannot be used to surveil people beyond their 
utterances in the conversation. 
Having established this background, it is worth describing 
what our system does not do, in order to clarify our choices. 
In particular, we emphasize that this is a minimalist system, 
which does not attempt to do everything, including 
recognizing users’ emotions throughout the conversation, 
identifying who talks the most or the least, and thus 
potentially being employed to identify toxic or abusive 
individuals. One could imagine such features being built, at 
least initially, using similar ASR and summarization 
components, but adding NLP elements, to analyze who 
contributes the most, thus potentially being abusive. Such 
features would turn our system into something else: a 
monitoring and controlling instrument, rather than a 
facilitation and coordination tool. Similarly, the moderation 
module does not attempt to profile individuals across 
conversations: it cannot identify toxic speakers, since it has 
no memory of what has been said in the past, beyond the 
current utterance. 
IV. PROPOSED SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE 
The platform is built with an event-driven, microservices-
first architecture in mind [25,26]: the frontend handles data 
ingestion and visualization, while the backend consists of a 
set of distributed services that perform AI inferencing. 
Specifically, each participant’s microphone input is sent to 
the server via WebRTC [12], split into 1-second WAV files, 
and passed to the FastAPI backend [27] via WebSockets. The 
latter routes the data to three processing nodes: 
ASR Node – Speech-to-Text and utterance-to-speaker 
diarization. 
Summarizer Node – Sliding-window algorithm for 
summary maintenance. 
Moderator Node – Intent classification to privately detect 
unprofessional language for the host. 
The three nodes are decoupled with an event bus (Kafka or 
Redis Streams) [13], which allows scaling and replacing 
individual elements of the pipeline with little to no downtime; 
for instance, one could swap out the summarizer model 
without affecting the ASR component. Storage is also 
decentralized: audio files are stored in object storage, 
transcripts – in a PostgreSQL database, whereas the 
summary, logs, and moderation alerts are in MongoDB. The 
dashboard, 
which 
presents 
transcripts, 
summaries, 
moderation alerts, and statistics, is served as a frontend app 
by the FastAPI backend via a REST API [27]. User auth is 
handled separately with OAuth 2.0/JWT [24]. The main 
advantage of the given approach is operational: individual 
models can be scaled or replaced independently of the overall 
pipeline, thus avoiding the downtime or complexity of 
rewriting the entire data ingestion system when, say, a new 
summarizer model appears. The data flow of the application, 
from the participants’ microphones to the host’s dashboard, 
is depicted in Fig. 1.

There are two levels of description of the data flow in this 
architecture. At Level 0, it is a process that communicates 
audio/video streams, meeting-control data, and moderation 
policies with the participants and hosts, writing them and 
reading from a single logical data store. At Level 1, it is 
decomposed into six services: ingestion of audio (raw), 
speech-to-text, 
speaker 
identification, 
NLP/summary, 
moderation, and dashboard/notifications. Each service has its 
storage (raw audio, text transcripts, speaker metadata, 
summary content, moderation alerts, and meeting/user 
metadata) and communicates with other models through their 
APIs (ASR, diarization, summarization, toxicity detection, 
notifications). 
Auto-scaling is performed at the Kubernetes pod level [14] 
(not model level). Each of the three main nodes for which the 
system is designed to handle bursts of concurrent meetings is 
implemented as a separate container with horizontal pod 
autoscaling. The burst is processed by adding more pods to 
all nodes, but the scaling policy prioritizes nodes so that the 
node which has the largest number of concurrent speakers 
(ASR node), the longest meeting (summarization node), or 
the number of messages (moderation node) gets more pods 
first and scales down last. The event bus handles the burst by 
letting moderation alerts queue until the dashboard can 
process them rather than dropping them outright. This is 
critical because the three nodes have different scales: one 
meeting has many speakers, but they speak one after another, 
giving a moderate load to the ASR node, and a bursty load to 
the moderation node, with summarization somewhere in 
between. It would be wasteful to scale all nodes 
simultaneously when, for example, ASR has headroom but 
the moderation node cannot keep up with the number of 
alerts. 
The dashboard is a thin client that subscribes to the REST 
API for the transcript, running summary, and moderation 
alerts. It updates the UI whenever new data is available rather 
than at fixed intervals, thus reducing the perceived latency on 
the screen to roughly the actual latency of the backend. It has 
logic to reconnect to the server and reload the transcript and 
running summary if the tab with the dashboard is closed 
during the meeting rather than losing the progress and having 
to reload from the beginning. This is essential because users 
frequently lose connection as their laptops move from one 
WiFi access point to another. 
Failures of any nodes are localized to that node by virtue of 
being in separate processes and having separate storage. If the 
moderation node crashes midway through the meeting, it 
does not affect the ASR and summarization nodes, and the 
dashboard will simply stop getting moderation alerts until it 
is reloaded. The same goes for failures of the summarization 
node; they are transparent to the other nodes. This is a general 
property of the microservices architecture that is expanded 
upon in Section VIII in contrast to a monolithic design, where 
such a cascade of failures would be much harder to prevent.

V. SECURITY, PRIVACY, AND DEPLOYMENT 
CONSIDERATIONS 
As it provides a service that listens to private conversations, 
the product has to prioritize security at the lowest level, not 
only on the application layer but also in the infrastructure. 
First, the audio content is protected by being transmitted via 
encrypted WebRTC and WebSocket, stored in the encrypted 
object storage and database with provider-managed keys. 
Second, the listening access, i.e. the ability to view the raw 
audio files, is partitioned from the transcript access, which is 
done to limit the attack surface in case of compromise of the 
dashboard. 
The configuration of consent and retention is done in the code 
as a policy because, for the described product, there is no 
other way to provide such a service – an explicit consent to 
record and analyze the conversation has to be provided before 
redirecting it to the ASR node, as it was explained in Section 
III. Additionally, the retention of the raw audio files, 

transcripts, and summaries can be set per organization per 
conversation, as after the retention deadline, the objects are 
to be deleted in accordance with the storage’s lifecycle 
policy. 
The moderation feed needs an extra explanation, as there is 
no personally identifiable information of a conversation 
participant in the moderation feed, including the transcript. 
Firstly, only the meeting host has access to the moderation 
feed by default, and there is no field that would allow 
extracting the PII of the identified participant from the object 
storage. Secondly, all actions with the objects, including raw 
audio files and transcript objects, as well as moderation-
related actions, are stored in a separate append-only audit log 
database that typically has to be presented and reviewed 
during a security or regulatory audit. 
The topology is also designed with the same security-centric 
approach in mind – to minimize exposure, which is why the 
product can be deployed in the jurisdiction of a particular 
organization if needed. For such deployment, it would be 
required to host the ASR, summarization, and moderation 
nodes inside the jurisdiction as well, as they do not rely on 
any external models or services at inference time. The 
reasoning behind it was explained in Section II – the 
local/quantized-inference topology is the only way to ensure 
low-latency ASR in the described product, which in turn has 
to be prioritized due to other architectural constraints. 
Therefore, the same topology can be used to satisfy the data 
residency requirements of a given jurisdiction without 
making any changes to the production models. On the other 
hand, if the organization is open to a managed model of 
service provisioning, the topology would be similar, but 
divided into several physical clusters that host multiple 
logical instances of the application with a network-level 
isolation between them. In any case, the information security 
controls would be similar on a per-tenant basis. 
VI. MATHEMATICAL FOUNDATION OF THE 
SUMMARIZATION MODULE 
The summarizer is based on Transformer self-attention [2]. 
Given query, key, and value matrices Q, K, V, with key 
dimensionality dₖ, scaled dot-product attention as used to 
construct contextual embeddings is: 
Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( (QKᵀ) / √dₖ ) V 
(1) 
which allows each token in a given sequence to compare 
itself with every other token via their embedding 
representations, thereby determining the relevance of each 
for computing a contextual representation. This is the 
fundamental mechanism that allows the summarizer to focus 
on specific parts of a conversation in order to distill their 
meaning, rather than, say, averaging over all tokens or using 
early or long tokens in the utterance. However, self-attention 
has a O(L²) complexity in the input length L, which would 
make the naive application of Eq. 1 impossible within the 
two-second latency budget for a meeting transcription of 
length L. We therefore apply the sliding-window constraint 
of Section II, which limits the attention span of each head and 
hence reduces the effective computational complexity to 
O(L) within the budget. 
The summarization backbone, depicted in Fig. 1, consists of 
multiple attention heads, each of which follows the same 
functional form as in Eq. 1, but with different trainable 
projections, applied in parallel to detect different patterns in 
the data. 
MultiHead(Q, K, V) = Concat(head₁, …, headₕ) Wᴼ 
(2) 
where each headᵢ = Attention(QWᵢQ, KWᵢK, VWᵢV), and 
Concat indicates the concatenation operation over the 
attention heads, and Wᴼ is a trainable projection matrix that 
takes the concatenated output of all heads as input. Note that 
the same sliding-window constraint of Section II applies to 
each individual attention head, as a single head that violates 
it would incur too much computational overhead. Indeed, the 
heads’ O(L²) time complexity, as seen in Eq. 1, cannot be 
offset by the parallel processing of Eq. 2. 
VII. PROPOSED EVALUATION METHODOLOGY 
The evaluation plan is based on standard corpora available to 
the public, not a custom dataset: AMI Meeting Corpus [9] 
and LibriSpeech ASR benchmark [10] for the ASR 
component, and Jigsaw Toxic Comment dataset [11] for the 
moderation. For raw audio, the resampling to 16 kHz mono 
and voice activity detection to remove silence before feeding 
the input to the model are examples of possible preprocessing 
steps, while the tokenization of text with byte-pair encoding 
would be another [1]. The model component for moderation 
is 
expected 
to 
be 
evaluated 
on 
an 
80/10/10 
train/validation/test split, with the NLP modules fine-tuned 
with AdamW and cross-entropy loss: learning rate 2e-5, 
batch size 16-32, and 3-5 epochs, with the stopping criteria to 
choose the epoch with the lowest validation loss for 
reporting. 
Three evaluation metrics are proposed, with the component 
in charge of each being the one that would benefit most from 
its optimization. First is the WER (Word Error Rate) [7,10], 
which is a standard metric for evaluating the performance of 
the automatic speech recognition component. Then, for the 
summarizer, the ROUGE-N and ROUGE-L metrics [6] 
would be used to approximate how much of human-level 
quality the model’s summaries have in terms of n-grams 
overlap and longest common subsequence. Finally, for the 
moderation component, the F1 score, harmonic mean of 
precision and recall, is suggested: 
F1 = 2 × Precision × Recall / (Precision + Recall) 
(3) 
The F1 score is a better choice than accuracy as a metric to 
report here. For binary classification, accuracy is a known 
improper scoring function, meaning that its optimization can 
lead to undesirable outcomes. In particular, for imbalanced 
datasets, which are expected to be the case for most meeting 

transcripts, any classifier that always predicts the negative 
class will have very high accuracy. 
Besides the primary evaluation metrics, an ablation study and 
a significance check are also proposed. The reason for the 
former is to ensure that the reported results are indeed driven 
from the architecture choices proposed and not from a 
particular combination of hyperparameters or training data 
choices. It would involve removing particular design choices 
to the extent possible, for example, using the summarizer 
without a sliding window, diarization without low-
dimensional embeddings and a stronger baseline, and 
replacing DistilBERT with a keyword filter for moderation. 
This way, the contribution of each change can be identified 
independently from the others. The reason for the latter is that 
the evaluation sets are coming from a public corpus with 
known variability between subsamples, so the differences 
between configurations should be checked for statistical 
significance rather than reported as a point estimate. 
Specifically, a paired significance test is suggested, and only 
a difference between two configurations that is significant at 
the 5% level should be labeled as such, to avoid claiming an 
effect that was caused by chance.

VIII. DISCUSSION: STRENGTHS AND OPEN 
CHALLENGES 
The main advantage of the architecture described by the 
authors stems from the design choices that address the 
shortcomings 
of 
the 
existing 
solutions 
while 
not 
contradicting the context of the task. Rather than trying to 
build upon the high-level ideas and scale them up, the authors 
tried to apply Occam’s Razor and improved several 
individual nodes in the pipeline, replacing post-hoc 
summarization with chunk-based streaming [28,29,30], cloud 
API with local/quantized inference [1,15], diarization with 
lightweight embeddings [8,19], and keyword filtering with a 
finetuned classifier [5,20]. Not less importantly, the decision 
to make the software a non-invasive overlay to existing 
conferencing platforms, not a replacement for them, was 
logical and contributed to the practicality of the solution, as 
adopting the former would be significantly easier for the 
companies that would have to implement it. 
Nevertheless, several design decisions made by the authors 
had to be considered as potential problems for the time being, 
as they may raise issues before the software is deployed. First 
of all, the decision to process ASR, summarization, and 
moderation on the same GPU without incurring extra latency 
costs is not devoid of risks and complications, especially as 
the concurrency level increases. Secondly, the choice to 
implement the sliding-window approach and sacrifice long-
term memory for lower latency and higher throughput comes 
with opportunity costs, as the snippets generated by the 
model can only represent the information contained within 
them. Thirdly, the expectation of F1 > 0.95 seems overly 
optimistic, given that, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, 
most of the Jigsaw data is text-based, and there is no 
guarantee of a considerable overlap between text and speech. 
Fourthly, the target audience of non-conference calls with 
English speech seems relatively narrow, which raises 
questions about the practical applicability of the software and 
its ability to generalize. 
A. Comparison to Alternative Architectures 
It is also worth discussing alternatives, both to the solutions 
described in the paper and to the design choices made by the 
authors. In terms of architectures, a monolithic inference 
pipeline is an option, as opposed to the current design that 
relies on multiple microservices. Overall, it is hard to say 
whether this approach is better, as it sacrifices flexibility and 
the ability to scale individual pipelines independently for 
lower overhead and, consequently, lower glass-to-glass 
latency. The latter, in particular, would benefit from being 
able to move lower through the stack, bypassing the 
unnecessary CPU/GPU cycles spent in the microservices 
layer [25,26], but the flexibility and control afforded by the 
current solution are unquestionable advantages, especially in 
the early stages of development. If the initial assumptions 
concerning the 2-second budget were relaxed in favor of sub-
200 millisecond latency, these considerations would be even 
more pertinent, and the reevaluation of the architectural 
decision would be necessary. 
Another hypothetical alternative concerns the distribution of 
the software. Specifically, the paper describes the use of 

server-side GPUs, but it is possible to imagine an edge-
focused discovery platform where every laptop would run the 
application locally rather than relying on the cloud. While 
such an approach would alleviate the scheduling costs 
described by the authors and resolve the concurrency and 
reliability issues, it would come at the cost of reduced 
performance, especially on machines without a discrete GPU. 
The variance in the performance would be significantly 
higher, which would undermine the reliability and the ability 
to meet the constraints described in Section I. Therefore, the 
server-side approach outlined in Section IV would be a safer 
option in this capacity.

summarization, and AI moderation in an event-driven 
architecture. For each detail, the review elaborates on the 
reasoning behind the choices based on the limitations 
mentioned in the paper and articulates the specific goals for 
latency, transcription quality, summary, and moderation 
beyond which the alternative would have been unsatisfactory. 
The next step in the project would be to implement the 
Speech/Multimodal AI Model-based ASR and diarization 
pipeline 
[1,8], 
sliding-window 
BART/T5-based 
summarization node [3,4,30], and DistilBERT-based fine-
tuned 
moderation 
classifier 
[5], 
connected 
through 
WebRTC/WebSockets [12], with model quantization for 
lower latency [15], benchmarked on the AMI Meeting 
Corpus and Jigsaw Toxic Comment datasets [9,11], and 
deployed through containers in the cloud [14]. Testing the 
entire pipeline’s end-to-end performance and the ability to 
keep the glass-to-glass latency within the expected limits 
would be the primary way of validating the hypothetical ideas 
about performance in real-life meetings. 
If successful, the next logical step would be to port the proof-
of-concept into the production environment, gradually 
exposing it to more users while keeping the majority within 
the company. First, it could be tested as a tool used by the 
development team, which would make the most sense to 
evaluate the accuracy and usability before moving on to a 
limited number of external partners as suggested in Section 
V. This way, the most pressing concerns could be addressed, 
such as what the host-facing Moderation Dashboard would 
be used for, and what the accuracy of the summaries and 
moderation would be in practice. Meeting the targets set in 
Section I would be the ultimate criterion for progressing 
towards the next stage and eventually releasing the product 
to the larger audience.
