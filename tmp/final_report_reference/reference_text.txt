ABSTRACT  
  
Healthcare records are often distributed across hospitals and clinics, while combining them in a 
central repository can increase privacy, governance, and fairness risks. This project presents 
FedFairChain, a privacy-conscious federated-learning framework for multi-label chronic-disease 
prediction. The framework enables participating healthcare clients to train a shared model while 
retaining raw patient records within their own authorized environments. Each client performs 
local data preparation, handles missing values, encodes categorical variables, scales numerical 
features, and trains a multi-label prediction model using its approved records. Only validated 
model updates and necessary training metadata are sent to the coordinating component for 
aggregation. 
The proposed workflow combines federated model training with client-level performance 
evaluation, fairness analysis, model-version management, and audit recording. The system 
supports prediction of multiple chronic-disease outcomes for the same patient record, reflecting 
the fact that chronic conditions may occur together. Performance is evaluated using suitable 
multi-label measures such as Hamming loss, precision, recall, F1-score, and per-label results. 
Fairness evaluation examines differences across participating clients or predefined groups instead 
of relying only on one overall score. 
FedFairChain is intended as an academic research prototype for privacy-conscious collaborative 
learning and decision support. It does not transfer raw patient records, provide a confirmed 
medical diagnosis, or claim formal privacy and fairness guarantees unless those properties are 
separately implemented and measured. The final conclusions are limited to the selected dataset, 
client configuration, model settings, and experimentally measured results. 
Keywords: Federated learning, multi-label classification, chronic-disease prediction, fairness 
evaluation, privacy-preserving machine learning, healthcare analytics, auditability.

CHAPTER 1
INTRODUCTION
1.1 Introduction
Healthcare institutions collect valuable patient information, including medical history, diag-
noses, laboratory reports, treatment records, and demographic attributes. This information
can be useful for identifying disease patterns and supporting early intervention. However,
chronic diseases such as diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and other related
conditions may occur together in the same patient. Therefore, the prediction task should
not always be treated as a single-label classification problem.
Multi-label classification enables a model to predict more than one disease label for a patient
record. This approach is useful in healthcare because it represents the possibility of multiple
coexisting conditions. A multi-label prediction model can help healthcare professionals
identify related disease risks and understand complex patterns within patient data. However,
reliable prediction requires sufficiently diverse data from different healthcare institutions.
Traditional machine-learning systems usually require all healthcare records from different
organizations to be collected in one central location. Although this can increase the amount
of available data, it also creates privacy, security, and governance concerns. Raw patient
records may contain confidential information that hospitals cannot share freely because of
institutional policies, patient confidentiality, and data-protection requirements.
Federated learning provides a privacy-conscious solution for collaborative model training.
In a federated-learning system, each participating healthcare institution keeps its data lo-
cally. The institution trains a model using its own authorized records and shares only per-
mitted model updates with a coordinating server. The server aggregates the updates to
improve a shared global model without directly accessing the raw patient data.
FedFairChain is proposed as a privacy-preserving federated-learning framework for multi-
label chronic disease prediction. The system combines federated learning, fairness-aware
evaluation, and traceable audit records. It is designed to support collaboration between
distributed healthcare institutions while preserving local ownership of sensitive patient in-
formation.
The proposed system also considers fairness because good overall prediction performance
does not always mean that the model performs equally well for every hospital or patient
group. A smaller institution, an underrepresented group, or a disease label with fewer
training examples may receive weaker predictions. Therefore, FedFairChain evaluates both
overall model performance and performance differences across participating clients or de-
fined groups.
The audit component of FedFairChain records important activities such as training rounds,

1.2 Problem Statement
Chronic diseases create a significant burden for patients, families, healthcare providers, and
public-health systems. Early identification of disease risk can support timely monitoring,
intervention, and treatment planning. However, effective prediction models require diverse
datasets, and such data are commonly distributed across multiple healthcare institutions.
Centralized data collection creates several challenges. Raw patient records may contain
sensitive information that cannot be exchanged freely because of privacy requirements, in-
stitutional policies, and data-governance restrictions. As a result, hospitals may be unable
to collaborate even when combining their knowledge would improve the quality of a pre-
diction model.
Healthcare data are also not identical across institutions. Different hospitals may have dif-
ferent patient populations, disease prevalence, sample sizes, clinical procedures, and data
quality. These differences can affect the performance of a global model. A model that
performs well for one institution may not perform equally well for another institution.
A further concern is fairness. A system that reports only a single overall accuracy value
may hide weaker performance for a particular hospital or patient group. Such unequal
results are important in healthcare because they can affect the reliability and usefulness of
the prediction model for different users.
There is therefore a need for a system that supports collaborative multi-label chronic disease
prediction without requiring raw patient data to leave the source institution. The system
must support local training, controlled update sharing, global aggregation, fairness analysis,
and auditable record keeping.
FedFairChain addresses this requirement by integrating federated learning, multi-label clas-
sification, fairness-aware evaluation, and audit logging. The prototype aims to reduce de-
pendence on centralized data collection while providing a transparent and traceable work-
flow for collaborative model development. The prediction output is intended only for aca-
demic research and decision-support purposes.
1.3 Objectives
• Develop a federated-learning workflow for multi-label chronic disease prediction.
• Ensure that raw patient records remain within their respective institutions.
• Train local models and aggregate permitted updates into a global model.
• Evaluate the model using suitable multi-label classification metrics.

CHAPTER – 2
LITERATURE SURVEY
The proposed Federated Classifier-Chain Model combines multi-label chronic disease pre-
diction, federated learning, fairness-aware evaluation, and auditable collaboration. This
chapter reviews research related to these areas. The review focuses on existing approaches,
their contributions, limitations, and relevance to the proposed system.
2.1 Multi-Label Chronic Disease Prediction
Multi-label classification is used when a single record can belong to more than one class at
the same time. In healthcare, patients may experience multiple chronic diseases simultane-
ously. Therefore, multi-label models are more suitable than traditional single-label models
for representing coexisting conditions.
Classifier chains model relationships between output labels instead of predicting each label
independently. In the proposed system, the classifier chain predicts diabetes first, followed
by cardiovascular disease and hypertension. The output of an earlier classifier can be used
as additional information for the following classifier.
Several studies have applied multi-label learning to disease prediction, clinical coding,
drug-resistance prediction, and chronic-condition analysis. These studies demonstrate that
correlated disease labels should be considered during model development.
Multi-label prediction is relevant to this project because one patient may have more than
one chronic condition. The proposed model therefore produces three related outputs instead
of forcing the patient into only one disease category.
2.2 Federated Learning in Healthcare
Federated learning enables multiple clients to train a shared model without transferring raw
data to a centralized server. Each participating client trains a local model using its own
approved dataset and sends only permitted model updates to the coordinating component.
This method is especially useful in healthcare because medical records contain sensitive
personal and clinical information. Hospitals may have different patient populations, disease
prevalence, sample sizes, and data-collection procedures. These differences create non-
identically distributed client data.
Federated learning allows institutions to collaborate while retaining local data ownership.
The coordinating component validates the submitted updates and combines them to produce
an improved global model.
The proposed system uses simulated healthcare clients to represent distributed institutions.

2.3 Fairness-Aware Machine Learning
Overall accuracy alone may not show whether a model performs equally across different
clients or patient groups. A model may achieve good average performance while producing
weaker results for a smaller institution, underrepresented group, or less frequent disease
label.
Fairness-aware machine learning evaluates performance separately for relevant groups. Com-
mon measures include precision, recall, F1-score, false-positive rate, false-negative rate,
and the difference between the best and worst group performance.
In a federated healthcare setting, fairness evaluation is important because client datasets
may differ in size, demographic composition, and label distribution. The proposed system
therefore reports both overall performance and client-level or group-level performance.
The fairness analysis in this project is an evaluation layer. It does not claim formal fairness
guarantees or prove that the system is clinically suitable for deployment.
2.4 Auditability and Explainable Artificial Intelligence
Auditability refers to the ability to trace how a model was trained, validated, updated, and
evaluated. In a federated system, audit records may include the training round, model ver-
sion, participating clients, update-validation result, aggregation status, performance mea-
sures, and fairness results.
Audit logging improves transparency because previous decisions and model versions can
be reviewed. It also helps identify whether an update was accepted, rejected, or sent for
further review.
Explainable artificial intelligence helps users understand which input features influenced
a model prediction. Explainability can be useful in healthcare decision-support systems
because users may require an interpretable reason for a predicted disease label.
The proposed system focuses mainly on traceable audit recording and human-readable pre-
diction output. Advanced explanation methods such as SHAP or LIME may be added when
required by the experimental design.
2.5 Privacy-Preserving Collaborative Learning
Centralized machine-learning systems require records from multiple institutions to be col-
lected in one location. This may create privacy, security, governance, and data-ownership
concerns.
Federated learning reduces the need for direct raw-data exchange because training occurs
locally. However, federated learning by itself does not automatically provide complete
privacy protection. Additional methods such as secure aggregation, differential privacy,

2.6 Summary of Research Gaps
The reviewed literature shows that multi-label classification is suitable for predicting several
related chronic diseases. Federated learning can support collaboration between distributed
healthcare institutions without requiring centralized raw records.
However, many existing studies focus mainly on prediction accuracy. Important issues
such as client heterogeneity, fairness, auditability, update validation, and transparent model
evaluation are not always addressed together.
The following research gaps are identified:
• Limited integration of multi-label disease prediction with federated learning.
• Insufficient evaluation of performance differences between clients.
• Limited recording of model versions, training rounds, and update decisions.
• Inadequate explanation of how submitted model updates are validated.
• Limited reporting of fairness gaps along with overall model performance.
• Lack of a complete workflow combining prediction, fairness, and auditability.
The proposed Federated Classifier-Chain Model addresses these gaps by combining multi-
label disease prediction, local client training, federated aggregation, fairness evaluation, and
audit recording. Raw data remain at the participating client, while only approved model
updates and required metadata are exchanged.

SYSTEM REQUIREMENT SPECIFICATION
This chapter presents the system requirement specification for the proposed FedFairChain
framework. It describes the overall system, product perspective, product functions, user
classes, design constraints, assumptions, hardware requirements, software requirements,
functional requirements, and non-functional requirements.
3.1 Overall Description
3.1.1 Product Perspective
FedFairChain is an AI-based healthcare prediction system designed to support multi-label
chronic disease prediction using federated learning. The system is intended for use by mul-
tiple participating healthcare institutions or data clients. Each client stores and processes its
own authorized data locally.
The proposed system contains local client modules, a coordinating server, a model aggrega-
tion component, a fairness evaluation component, and an audit record component. The local
client performs data preprocessing and model training. The coordinator manages training
rounds and receives approved model updates from participating clients.
The raw patient records remain within the local institution. Only permitted model param-
eters, evaluation summaries, or other approved information are transmitted to the coordi-
nating component. The global model is created by combining valid local updates from the
participating clients.
The system is designed as a research prototype. It supports model development, evalua-
tion, fairness analysis, and auditability. It does not replace clinical diagnosis or provide
independent medical treatment recommendations.
3.1.2 Product Functions
The primary functions of the FedFairChain system are:
1. Client Registration: Register participating healthcare institutions and maintain infor-
mation about their authorized participation in the federated-learning process.
2. Local Data Preparation: Allow each client to clean, encode, normalize, and prepare
its local data without transferring raw patient records to the coordinating server.
3. Local Model Training: Train the prediction model using the local client dataset and
the model configuration provided for the current training round.

CHAPTER – 5
SYSTEM DESIGN
The system design describes the complete workflow of the Federated Classifier-Chain Model
for Multi-Label Chronic Disease Prediction. It includes local data preparation, feature
processing, federated model training, multi-label prediction, fairness evaluation, and au-
dit recording. Raw patient records remain within participating clients, while only approved
model information is exchanged.
5.1 Architectural Diagram
The proposed system follows a distributed healthcare-learning architecture in which patient
data are retained by the participating client. The system contains a local client layer, a coor-
dination layer, a federated aggregation layer, a fairness-evaluation layer, and an authorized
prediction interface.
At the client layer, each participating healthcare institution loads its authorized patient
records and performs all preprocessing operations locally. Missing values are handled,
categorical attributes are converted into a suitable numerical representation, and numerical
features are scaled using the selected preprocessing configuration. The processed data are
then supplied to the local classifier-chain model.
The coordination layer manages client registration, model distribution, training rounds, up-
date validation, and communication with participating clients. Before an update is accepted,
the coordinator verifies the client identity, model version, parameter format, feature com-
patibility, and required metadata.
The federated aggregation layer combines only valid client updates. Raw patient records
and local feature matrices are not transferred to the coordinator. The resulting global model
is distributed to approved clients for the next training round.
The fairness and audit layers operate after model aggregation. The fairness layer compares
performance across clients or predefined groups. The audit layer stores the training round,
model version, update-validation result, aggregation status, performance values, and fair-
ness results.
This architecture separates data ownership from model collaboration. It allows participating
institutions to contribute to a shared model while maintaining local control over their patient
records.

The proposed model is designed for a multi-label healthcare prediction task. Unlike a

5.9 Fairness Evaluation and Audit Layer
The fairness layer evaluates whether the model produces comparable results across partic-
ipating clients or selected patient groups. The evaluation does not depend only on overall
accuracy. It also considers label-specific precision, recall, F1-score, false-positive rate,
false-negative rate, and the difference between group-level results.
The fairness gap is calculated by comparing the best and worst observed group performance
for a selected metric. A large gap indicates that the model should be examined further before
its results are used for decision support.
The audit layer maintains a chronological record of important system events. Each record
may contain the training-round number, model version, participating client identifiers, update-
validation status, aggregation status, performance metrics, fairness results, and authorized
actions.
Audit records allow researchers and administrators to trace how a particular model version
was produced. If an update is rejected or a fairness result requires review, the decision is
stored as a new event rather than silently changing an earlier record.

6.1 Methodology
6.1.1 Overall Workflow
The proposed workflow begins with the preparation of approved patient records at partici-
pating healthcare clients. Raw records remain within their local environments. Each client
applies the common preprocessing pipeline, trains its local classifier-chain model, and cre-

The coordinator validates the received updates and forwards valid updates to the feder-

The final global model is used by the authorized prediction interface. Input features are pro-

produces probabilities and final labels for diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and hyperten-
sion.
6.1.2 Dataset Description
The dataset contains approved and de-identified patient records. Each record contains de-
mographic, body-measurement, laboratory, and lifestyle features. The target is multi-label
because one patient may have more than one chronic condition.
The same feature order and label order are used by all clients. Direct identifiers are removed
before model training. The dataset is divided into training, validation, and testing data
according to the experimental protocol.

6.1.3 Data Preprocessing
The preprocessing pipeline contains the following operations:
1. Validate required input fields.
2. Remove direct patient identifiers.
3. Handle missing values using the training-fitted imputation strategy.
4. Encode categorical variables such as Smoking Status and Physical Activity.
5. Scale numerical variables using training-data parameters only.
6. Validate numerical ranges and feature types.

6.1.6 Classifier-Chain Methodology
The model uses a classifier-chain approach for multi-label prediction. A separate binary
classifier is used for each disease label. The first classifier predicts diabetes. Its output is
then supplied as additional chain information to the cardiovascular-disease classifier. The
hypertension classifier uses the original feature representation together with the preceding
classifier outputs.
This method allows the model to represent relationships between disease labels. The out-
put probabilities are converted into binary predictions using the configured label-specific
thresholds.
6.1.7 Local Client Training
Four simulated clients are used to represent participating healthcare institutions. Each client
receives or retains its own local data partition. Local preprocessing is performed without
transferring raw records.
During a training round, the client receives the current global model, trains the local classifier-
chain model, evaluates the local result, and creates a permitted model update. The update
contains only the information required by the aggregation protocol.
6.1.8 Federated Aggregation
The coordinator receives model updates from eligible clients. Each update is checked for
client identity, model version, parameter shape, feature compatibility, and required meta-
data.
Only valid updates are included in federated averaging. The resulting global model is as-
signed a new model version and distributed during the next training round. Raw patient
records are not included in the aggregation process.
6.1.9 Fairness Evaluation
Fairness evaluation compares performance across participating clients or predefined groups.
The evaluation may include precision, recall, F1-score, false-positive rate, false-negative
rate, and the difference between the best and worst group results.
The fairness gap is recorded together with the group sizes, disease-label prevalence, and
evaluation configuration. Fairness evaluation provides diagnostic information and does not
by itself establish clinical fairness.

are distributed across multiple institutions and raw records must remain under the control
of their original data holders.
The completed workflow includes local data preparation, missing-value handling, categorical-
feature processing, numerical-feature scaling, classifier-chain training, controlled update
validation, federated aggregation, performance evaluation, fairness analysis, and audit record-
ing.
Each participating client prepares its approved data locally and trains the configured model
without transferring raw patient records to the coordinating server. Only approved model
updates and required training metadata are submitted for aggregation.
The model supports multi-label prediction for Diabetes, Cardiovascular Disease, and Hy-
pertension. A single patient record can receive more than one positive disease label, which
is suitable for representing the coexistence of chronic conditions.
The recorded disease-specific results show comparable centralized and federated perfor-
mance. Diabetes accuracy was recorded as 92.23% for the centralized model and 92.16%
for the federated model. Hypertension accuracy was recorded as 70.85% for the centralized
model and 70.32% for the federated model. CVD accuracy was recorded as 87.98% for
both settings.
The recorded precision values were 88.19% and 88.71% for centralized and federated Dia-
betes prediction, 65.11% and 65.73% for Hypertension prediction, and 53.49% and 53.85%
for CVD prediction, respectively. These values indicate that the federated model produced
results comparable to the centralized model for the available disease-specific measures.
Fairness evaluation was performed using age group and sex as audited attributes. The avail-
able evaluation records included six centralized group results, six federated group results,
and 54 recorded comparisons. Observed differences included a Diabetes age-group recall
change of +0.3029, a Hypertension age-group specificity change of -0.1521, and a CVD
age-group AUROC change of -0.1388.

validation decisions, aggregation events, performance metrics, and fairness results. This

prototype and must not be interpreted as a confirmed clinical diagnostic system.
The final conclusions are limited by the selected dataset, the number and composition of

complete round-wise results must be reported only after they are verified from the original
evaluation files.
9.2
Future Scope
Several improvements can be considered to strengthen the proposed model and extend it to
larger research studies.
• Secure aggregation: Future versions can implement secure aggregation so that the
coordinator cannot inspect individual client updates before combining them.
• Differential privacy: Differential-privacy mechanisms can be evaluated to reduce
the risk of information leakage from model updates while measuring their effect on
prediction performance.
• Larger healthcare datasets: Future studies should use larger, more diverse, and
better-validated healthcare datasets with broader demographic and clinical coverage.
• More participating institutions: The system can be evaluated with a larger number
of real or simulated healthcare institutions to study client heterogeneity, communica-
tion cost, and client dropout.
• External validation: The final model should be tested on an independent dataset
collected from a different institution or under a different acquisition protocol.
• Personalized federated learning: Personalized federated-learning methods can be
studied so that each institution receives a model adapted to its local population while
still benefiting from global knowledge.
• Improved class-imbalance handling: Future work can evaluate class weighting, cali-
brated thresholds, resampling, cost-sensitive learning, and additional imbalance-aware
methods.
• Explainable artificial intelligence: SHAP, LIME, feature-importance analysis, and
example-based explanations can be added to help researchers and authorized users
understand prediction results.

• Improved fairness analysis: Future experiments should define clinically meaning-
ful groups, report confidence intervals, evaluate additional protected attributes, and
analyse fairness under client imbalance and missing data.
• Real-world clinical validation: Before any practical use, the model must undergo ex-
ternal clinical validation, ethical review, security assessment, monitoring, and human
expert evaluation.
• Mobile deployment: A lightweight mobile interface may be developed for authorized
healthcare workers operating in locations with limited computing resources.
• Cloud deployment: The system may be deployed using secure cloud infrastructure
with controlled access, encrypted communication, model monitoring, and scalable
federated coordination.
• Clinical governance: Future deployment should include consent management, access
control, incident reporting, model monitoring, retraining policies, and clear human
oversight.
The future direction of the project is to improve privacy protection, validate the model
across independent populations, strengthen fairness measurement, handle class imbalance,
improve explainability, and establish responsible governance for real-world use. All future
claims should be supported by reproducible experiments and verified evidence.
